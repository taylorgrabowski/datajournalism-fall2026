## Lede
This story has two ledes, starting with a scrolling visual intro that shows two houses in Kansas City, Missouri, only a 0.4-mile walk apart, with one offered internet at 300 megabits per second and the other at 5. The graphic shows that the slower house is in a poorer area with fewer White residents, that both pay the same $55 a month, and that this is happening in 38 cities. The written story then opens with Shirley Neville, a New Orleans resident whose internet kept cutting out during meetings and who was told by AT&T that nothing could be done. A few paragraphs later, AT&T offers speeds almost 400 times faster for the same $55 in Lakeview, a richer, mostly White neighborhood. I think this was effective because the visual intro lets the reader see the problem and its scale before any analysis, the Neville anecdote puts a real person behind it, and the Lakeview comparison makes the unfairness easy to understand. 
I would change a few things. The paragraph about Neville's speeds has three numbers (1, 25 and 167 Mbps) before the reader knows what a megabit is, and the paragraph explaining what the reporters did (gathering more than 800,000 offers) doesn't appear until about eight paragraphs into the text, so I would move it up. The headline is also a little confusing, since "paying 400 times as much as your neighbor" sounds like different bills when the point is that people pay the same price for very different speeds.


## The Data
The reporters collected nearly 850,000 internet offers from AT&T, Verizon, EarthLink and CenturyLink in the largest city in 38 states, using the address lookup tools on the companies' own websites. They compared the speeds and prices to Census data on income and race and to 1930s redlining maps that marked some neighborhoods as "hazardous" for loans. 
- All four companies offered some neighborhood speeds of 200 Mbps or more for the same price as speeds below 25 Mbps in other neighborhoods.
- The worst offers went to lower-income areas in nine out of 10 cities.
- The worst offers went to the least-White areas in two-thirds of the cities with enough data.
- The worst offers went to formerly redlined areas in all 22 cities where the reporters could find the old maps.

The text never clearly defines a "worst deal" (the chart notes say "slow" means under 25 Mbps), and the data covers offers rather than what people actually pay, which AT&T pointed out by saying the story ignored its low-income plans and a federal discount program. The reporters responded with research showing few people sign up for the discount, but we still don't know what customers really pay. They only looked at the largest city in each state, it's hard to say this applies everywhere, and the density test appears to have been run only for AT&T, so I wondered if the other companies would hold up. Finally, the data shows a pattern but can't say why it exists, since the companies say it is about cost and critics say it is a choice not to invest in some neighborhoods.

## Numbers
Most of the writing is easy to follow, with phrases like "nine out of 10 cities," "two-thirds" and "almost eight times as often," and the story moves the heaviest numbers into charts to keep the text lighter. The CenturyLink paragraph has about 14 digits in two sentences (200 Mbps for as little as $0.25 per Mbps, and 0.5 Mbps for 400 times as much, or $100 per Mbps), and it could say something like "CenturyLink charged some customers pennies for each unit of speed and others 400 times more" and let the chart show the exact prices. That might be a bit heavy for readers to easily digest. The Kansas City numbers (68 percent vs. 12 percent) could be "more than five times as often," and the story never explains what a megabit is, which would help readers who aren't tech-savvy.


## Data Visualization
The data visualizations do a lot of useful work in this story. The scrolling intro is the best part because each step adds one idea (the speeds, the distance, income and race, then the price), so the reader understands the main point without a number-heavy paragraph. A few things are more decoration than information, like the wavy lines around the houses and the two separate $55 panels that could have been one sentence, but they don't take up much space. The price chart (price per megabit for each company) takes the heavy numbers out of the CenturyLink paragraph, but it only shows each company's best and worst deal, so a reader can't tell how often someone gets the worst one. The Las Vegas bar chart is clear, with direct labels, and shows that 68 percent of CenturyLink's offers in lower-income areas were slow compared with 41 percent in upper-income areas. But the text right after it says more than 80 percent of offers in Diaz's district were slow, which could confuse readers, and the 41 percent number isn't mentioned in the text. The New Orleans bar chart is simple and shows 77 percent of the least-White areas were offered slow internet compared with 45 percent of the most-White areas. However, 45 percent is also high and the story doesn't discuss it, so the Lakeview comparison in the text is the best-case example, not the typical one. The two charts that cover all the cities are the hardest to read. The income map uses squares for cities but most aren't labeled, so I couldn't find my own city, and its color key measures the gap in percentage points while the one note says Oklahoma City had "more than 7 times" as many poor households with slow internet, which is a different way of measuring. The race chart is a scatterplot with a diagonal band meaning "the difference was 5 points or less," which shows 38 cities at once but requires the reader to understand the band before the chart makes sense. The Kansas City maps are the strongest evidence, with the left map showing slow-speed addresses piled up in the redlined areas and the right map showing the fastest speeds mostly in the historically "best" areas. Having both side by side makes the 68 percent number easy to see, though the 12 percent comparison number from the text is missing from the map.


## Methods
The card in the middle of the story links to a separate article called "How We Uncovered Disparities in Internet Deals," which keeps the main story easy to read while giving curious readers a way to check the work. Inside the story, the reporters explain the basics, which is the data came from the companies' own websites, they used a method from Princeton researchers, they only looked at the largest city in each state, and they ran a test to answer AT&T's argument. I think this works, but a reader who doesn't click the link never learns exactly how a "worst deal" was defined, or that offers are not the same as what people actually pay. A short note in the story or a clearer pointer near the top would help, especially since the companies' main complaint was that the analysis was flawed.


## Etc
The story is from 2022, so I would like to know what happened after it ran, such as whether the FCC finished its rules on digital discrimination, whether the federal discount program continued, and whether any company changed its prices or built new service in the neighborhoods named. I also noticed that the story doesn't seem to give regular readers a way to check their own address or neighborhood, which would have made it more useful to the people in it. The story ends strongly by pointing out that Hope Village residents didn't know they were paying the same price as richer neighborhoods for worse service until the reporters told them, which shows why the reporting matters. It could have gone further to say what residents can do now that they know.
